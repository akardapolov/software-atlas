# Niklaus Wirth

| | |
|---|---|
| **Born** | 1934, Winterthur, Switzerland |
| **Died** | 2024 |
| **Fields** | Programming languages, compiler construction, hardware/software co-design |
| **Known for** | Pascal, Modula, Modula-2, Oberon, Oberon OS, structured programming |
| **Turing Award** | 1984 |

## Biography

Niklaus Wirth was a Swiss computer scientist and one of the most influential
language designers of the 20th century. He spent most of his career at ETH
Zurich, where he designed a sequence of languages — ALGOL W, Pascal, Modula-2,
and Oberon — each one smaller and more disciplined than the last.

Wirth was both a strong engineer and an exceptional educator. Colleagues
remembered him as modest in daily life but demanding in his work: he would
replace a hash table with a linear list if the problem did not justify the
complexity. His entire career was a sustained argument for **simplicity as an
engineering virtue**.

## Key Contributions

### Pascal (1970)

Wirth designed **Pascal** as a clean teaching language that embodied structured
programming. It became one of the most widely used languages for education and
professional development in the 1970s and 1980s.

### Modula and Modula-2 (1970s–1980)

**Modula-2** added modules to Pascal, giving programmers explicit control over
interfaces and encapsulation. It influenced later systems languages and
object-oriented extensions.

### Oberon (1987)

**Oberon** was Wirth's most radical simplification. It stripped away features
that were considered essential — conventional classes, even the `FOR` loop — and
added garbage collection to a systems language. Wirth's team then built custom
hardware (the Ceres workstation) and an entire operating system written almost
entirely in Oberon.

Oberon demonstrated that a complete, productive computing environment could be
built from a remarkably small language core.

### Wirth's Law

Wirth is also associated with **Wirth's Law**: "Software is getting slower more
rapidly than hardware becomes faster." It was his warning against the tendency
of software systems to absorb efficiency gains through unnecessary complexity.

## Influence

### Influenced by

- **ALGOL 60** — the structured-programming foundation of Wirth's early work.

### Influenced

- **Pascal family** — Turbo Pascal, Delphi, Ada, and many teaching languages.
- **Modula family** — Oberon, Component Pascal.
- **Java** — Sun licensed Oberon and Jbed materials, influencing the Java
  runtime.
- **.NET / CLR** — Clemens Szyperski's work on Component Pascal and Oberon
  influenced CLR design.
- **Go** — shares Oberon's emphasis on fast compilation, simplicity, and systems
  orientation.

## Why Wirth Matters

Wirth proved that removing features can be harder — and more valuable — than
adding them. His languages are case studies in how far minimalism can go while
remaining practical. The "Wirth line" of languages remains a touchstone for
anyone who believes that software should be understandable, predictable, and
small.
